The Hurricane Story

A Thanksgiving scene from before the story in Reversible begins. It isn’t in the book, and it spoils nothing.

Andrew cooks two things, and on Thanksgiving he is allowed one of them. The turkey is mine. The brisket is his, and he had been up since four tending it, which he mentioned to everyone who came through the door.

By two o’clock the house was full. Michael and Sarah had driven over with the children. Julia and Caleb had flown in from Colorado the night before. Nathan had come home from school with a duffel of laundry and the look of a young man who had not eaten a vegetable since August.

We held hands around the table, all ten of us, and Andrew prayed. He thanked God for the food and for the hands that made it, and for every person in the room by name, the way he has every year since there were only two names to say. Lily, who was seven, squeezed my fingers at hers.

Then we ate, and I waited. It came with the second helpings, as it always does.

“Did I ever tell you kids about the hurricane?”

“Yes,” said Cindy, who was thirteen.

“Corpus Christi. Two thousand eight.” He set down his fork. “The power’s out in the whole building. Water coming in under the door.”

“Last year the water wasn’t in the building,” Michael said.

“Last year I was being modest.”

I have heard this story for sixteen Thanksgivings. In the first telling, there was a storm and a long night and a machine that had to be mended before morning. Something called a failover cluster, which I have never understood and no longer try to. Since then the story has acquired rising water, a flashlight held in his teeth, and a man named Ray who held the ladder.

“Was there a ladder last year?” Ethan asked. He was eleven and kept track of things.

“There has always been a ladder.”

“What happened to Ray?” Lily asked.

“Ray is fine. I still owe Ray twenty dollars.” He looked down the table at me. “Tell them it’s true.”

“It gets better every year,” I said.

Everyone laughed, and he put his hand over his heart as if I had wounded him, and went on to the part where the lights come back.

What the children don’t know is what I remember of that night. I was at home with three of my own and the television on, watching the storm come ashore, and the phone did not ring until morning. I prayed for him the way you pray when there is nothing else to do. When he finally called, he said, “We got it running,” and then, “I’m sorry I couldn’t call,” and I sat down on the kitchen floor and cried from relief.

He has never put that part in the story. I don’t think he knows it belongs there.

After the pie, Sarah and Julia chased me out of my own kitchen, and I let them, which I would not have done ten years ago. Andrew found me on the back porch. The afternoon had gone gold over the yard, and his hives stood quiet along the fence.

“You could have backed me up on the ladder,” he said.

“I wasn’t there.”

“Forty years. You’re supposed to lie for me by now.”

“I told them it gets better every year. That was generous.”

He laughed and sat down beside me, a little stiffly, the way we both sit now. Through the window we could hear the children arguing over a board game, and somebody running water, and Nathan laughing at something Caleb said.

“That’s a lot of people,” Andrew said.

“We started it.”

“We did.” He took my hand. “I don’t know what I’d ask for that isn’t in that house.”

I didn’t either. I sat there holding his hand and thanked God for the noise.
